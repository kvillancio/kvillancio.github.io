Muscle-controlled ankle exoskeleton

Evaluating a powered ankle exoskeleton with proportional myoelectric control in both lab and real world settings

>

Abstract: Lower limb robotic exoskeletons are often studied in the context of steady state treadmill walking in a laboratory environment. However, the end goal for exoskeletons is to be used in real world, complex environments. To reach the point that exoskeletons are openly adopted into our everyday lives, we need to understand how the human and robot interact outside of a laboratory. Metabolic cost is often viewed as a gold standard metric for measuring exoskeleton performance but is rarely used to evaluate performance at non steady state walking outside of a laboratory. In this study, we tested the effects of robotic ankle exoskeletons under proportional myoelectric control on the cost of transport of walking both inside on a treadmill and outside overground. We hypothesized that walking with the exoskeletons would lead to a lower cost of transport compared to walking without them both on a treadmill and outside. We saw no significant increases or decreases in cost of transport or exoskeleton mechanics when walking with the exoskeletons compared to walking without them both on a treadmill and outside. We saw a strong negative correlation between walking speed and cost of transport when walking with and without the exoskeletons. In the future, research should consider how performing more difficult tasks, such as incline and loaded walking, affects the cost of transport while walking with and without robotic ankle exoskeletons. The value of this study to the literature is that it emphasizes the importance of both hardware dynamics and controller design towards reducing metabolic cost of transport with robotic ankle exoskeletons. When comparing our results to other studies using the same hardware with different controllers or very similar controllers with different hardware, there are a wide range of outcomes as to metabolic benefit.


Hybart, R., Villancio-Wolter, K. S., & Ferris, D. P. (2023). Metabolic cost of walking with electromechanical ankle exoskeletons under proportional myoelectric control on a treadmill and outdoors. PeerJ, 11, e15775. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.15775

Exoskeleton control parameter optimization

Using linear mixed models to characterize the effects of exoskeleton control parameters on various user outcomes

>

Abstract (paper in progress): Lower limb exoskeletons have a wide array of applications in mobility assistance and rehabilitation. Many exoskeleton control strategies exist and each is defined by a set of controller-specific parameters. However, within a given control strategy, we do not yet know how different exoskeleton control parameters affect features of human gait. To bridge this gap, we conducted a secondary analysis of an open source dataset (Poggensee et al., 2021). This dataset consisted of 15 participants in 3 training groups walking on a treadmill with bilateral powered ankle exoskeletons over 6 days. Exoskeleton assistance was determined by an event-triggered torque profile defined by four parameters: (1) peak torque magnitude, (2) peak torque time, (3) rise time, and (4) fall time. Each training group experienced a different approach to optimizing exoskeleton assistance. We explored the effects of each control parameter on human gait features (maximum dorsiflexion and plantarflexion angles, metabolic cost, and stride time) using linear mixed effects models (LMEMs). We also modeled the effects of different optimization approaches, experience, individual differences, and between-parameter interactions. If we can predict how control parameters affect different features of human gait, we can design controllers to achieve more precise goals, which can be leveraged for applications like user-customization and rehabilitation.

Inverse kinematics sensor pipeline comparison

Comparing inverse kinematics calculations using motion capture and inertial measurement units with varying placement configurations

>

Abstract: Inertial measurement units (IMUs) are wearable sensors that can be used for portable human gait analysis (e.g. joint angles). This has many potential applications, from clinical gait assessments to outdoor research studies. Our study aims to investigate how walking speed and IMU placement on the body effects joint angle calculations during gait. In this study, 4 healthy young adults wore 10 IMUs (APDM Opals) on their lower body along with full-body motion capture markers. The participants walked on a treadmill at 4 different speeds for 2 minutes each. We calculated their joint angles (hip, knee, and ankle) with the motion capture data (Visual 3D) and IMU data (OpenSense inverse kinematics). We compared the calculated joint angles in the sagittal plane at different walking speeds and with different IMU placement configurations. From preliminary results, we observed that IMU placement on the foot does have an effect on ankle angle calculations. Additionally, the IMU and motion capture joint angles had fewer regions of significant differences at 0.2 m/s compared to 1.0 m/s. For future directions, we will process a larger sample of participants and more walking speeds to build a more representative dataset. We will also process data from a stroke study to investigate IMU gait analysis with a clinical population.

Paper circuit art lesson!

Teaching introductory circuit concepts to elementary school students through art

>

During grad school, I had the opportunity to design and lead a lesson plan for an after-school STEM program at a local elementary school in Seattle. I knew I wanted to create a lesson anchored in art both because I believe art and engineering go hand in hand and because I noticed how much more engaged the students were during activities that allowed for creative ownership. I also knew I wanted to do a hands-on circuit activity because after six years of university-level engineering courses, I still owe my understanding of shorting a circuit to a stack of foam blocks, a pack of LEDs, and a roll of conductive tape. Tangible, large scale activities like this are a great way to teach circuit fundamentals to students at any level and I'm so grateful I got the opportunity to bring this one to the classroom.


I designed this activity based on this paper circuit guide. To introduce an additional circuit component, I modified the switch mechanism to magnetic reed switches. To close the circuit, students had to make an accessory to their art piece [in my demo, a heart for my robot] and attach a magnet to the back of it. When they hold the magnetic piece over the reed switch, the circuit closes and the LEDs illuminate.


Happy circuit building!
